# How to develop good research questions

Credit: Computational neuroscientist Megan A. K. Peters, published in Nature Human Behaviour.

## Phases

- Phase 1: Self-Critical Brainstorming & Speaking Aloud: Engaging in internal
  dialogue and verbalizing raw concepts to identify what genuinely drives your
  scientific curiosity.

- Phase 2: Context Building & Wide Reading: Reading broadly across disciplines
  and using manual thought-mapping tools to visualize conceptual connections.

- Phase 3: Distilling the Essence: Narrowing the focus down to core "what if"
  scenarios, deliberately reframing old assumptions, or showing how previous
  methods masked real understanding.

- Phase 4: The Final Targeted Question: Polishing the idea into a precise,
  operationalized inquiry ready for testing.

## Traps to Avoid

- Trap: The Hypothesis Requirement: Forcing a rigid hypothesis too early in the
  process, which can prematurely blind a researcher to alternative exploratory
  angles.

- Trap: Over-Attachment: Becoming so personally invested in a specific initial idea
  or expected outcome that you ignore contradictory evidence or better
  pathways.

- Trap: Prior Existence of an Idea: Assuming a research question is invalid simply
  because a similar concept has been touched upon before; valuable insight
  often comes from reimagining old problems.

- Trap: Hammer-and-Nail Bias: Letting your favorite tools, specific mathematical
  models, or lab equipment dictate the question, rather than choosing the right
  tools to serve the inquiry.
